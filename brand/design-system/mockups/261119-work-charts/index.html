Work charts: a programme's work, week by week

A programme's overview gains a tab, Work, between Plan and Changes. Work is weight × reps × sets; a marked day counts its session's full planned work; an activity without weight counts total reps, or seconds held. Two questions, each answered on its own. An activity's item page shows its own chart under the step chart, in whichever form is picked in question 1.

Beginner 5×5 in week 4 of 8, today Thu 19 Nov. Five weighted lifts, three sessions a week (the deadlift one), and a plank added in week 2, held three times a week. Weeks 4 and 8 are deloads. The plan's numbers are the programme's own.

Question 1 of 3 · decided: A

1. The chart

Each option draws the programme's total work, in kg, over its eight weeks. Over every chart a line says this week's work and the total so far: "Week 4: 2,400 kg of 5,650 kg planned. 29,775 kg done so far." Done is drawn in pen, the colour the marks are drawn in; this week sits on a tile band, as on the step chart.

A. A column per week: planned outlined, done filled

Option A

Each week is a column outlined at its planned work (1.5px ink-secondary; dashed control-border for the weeks to come), filled with pen up to the work done. A full column is a week done as planned; a deload is visibly shorter. Recommended: one glance says both how much was planned and how much of it was done, week by week.

B. Lines: planned as a step, done as dots

Option B

As the step chart draws an activity's weight: the planned work a step per week (solid ink to this week, dashed after), and a pen dot at each week's work done, joined by a thin pen line. Close to the chart already on the item page; a week done short is a dot below its step.

C. Running totals

Option C

Two lines rising across the programme: the work planned to date (ink, dashed after this week, ending at the programme's whole planned work), and the work done so far (pen). The gap between them is the work behind. Shows the whole programme's progress; a single week's shortfall is harder to see.

Question 2 of 3 · decided: A

2. The tab

Both options start with the programme's total, then show each activity in its own measure: the lifts in kg, the plank in seconds held. The total leaves out the plank and says so. Drawn with option A's columns; whichever form is picked applies.

A. The total, then a small chart for every activity

Option A

Under the total, "By activity" and a chart per activity, two to a row on a laptop, one to a row on a phone. Every activity is in view at once to compare. Recommended: nothing to choose, and the lifts line up.

B. The total, then one activity at a time

Option B

Under the total, a select, "Activity", choosing one activity, drawn as large as the total. Quieter, one chart at a time; comparing two lifts means switching.

Question 3 of 3 · new, after John's note

3. A long programme

Not every programme runs 8 weeks: one could run two years. Here a 104-week programme is in week 40. A column per week is about 12px wide on the whole programme's chart, and 3 to 6px on an activity's or a phone's. Both options keep the columns of question 1; they differ in what happens when the weeks don't fit. (Today a programme runs at most 52 weeks; two years would raise that to 104.)

A. A column per week, scrolling sideways

Option A

Each week keeps its own column, at least 18px apart. When they don't fit, the chart scrolls sideways inside itself, as the overview's grid does, and opens with this week in view; the axis stays put at the left. Every week stays exact; the rest of the programme is a scroll away.

B. Weeks grouped to fit

Option B

When weekly columns would be narrower than 14px, each column adds up two weeks, four or eight, whichever is the fewest that fits that chart: on a laptop the whole programme's chart shows weeks in twos; on a phone, eights. Labels name the span ("37–40"), and the column holding this week sits on the band. Pointing at a column, and the table a screen reader gets, still give each week. Recommended: the whole programme in view on every screen, nothing hidden off to the side.
